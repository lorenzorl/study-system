# Classifying a book

The classification is not a label; it decides **which retrieval test the reader has to
pass**. A book misclassified gets practice of the wrong kind: it feels productive and
fails quietly.

Pick **one dominant type**. Name a secondary tail only when it changes the plan.

## The four types

### Procedural — it teaches how to do something

**Signals:** numbered steps, before/after figures, commands, tool names, "never do X",
exercises with expected output. The reader is meant to be at a keyboard, a bench, a
kitchen.

**Retrieval test:** perform the procedure, closed book. Reading it again is not study.

**Plan consequence:** chapters become practice sessions, not reading sessions. The
output of a block is a thing that works, not a summary. Cards are "what do you do when
Y happens" and are nearly worthless until the procedure has been performed once.

### Conceptual — it gives criteria for judgment

**Signals:** patterns, definitions tied to a trade-off, "when to use / when not to use",
diagrams of relationships, a vocabulary that keeps referring to the same few ideas.
(Trade books, textbooks, design and economics.)

**Retrieval test:** apply the criterion to a **new** case. "What is an aggregate?" can
be answered by recognizing the book's sentence; "what defines an aggregate's boundary?"
cannot.

**Plan consequence:** cards of definition are close to useless. What works is judgment
cards plus one application per chapter, on the reader's own material. Without the
application step this type produces readers who can talk and cannot decide.

### Factual — it stores information

**Signals:** dense proper nouns, dates, lists, taxonomies, tables, a glossary that is
as important as the prose. (Anatomy, history surveys, pharmacology.)

**Retrieval test:** recall the datum, unprompted.

**Plan consequence:** this is the type where spaced repetition is nearly the whole
method, and where volume is legitimate. Still one fact per card, still no card that can
be answered by recognition.

### Argumentative — it defends a thesis

**Signals:** a claim in the introduction, chapters that answer objections, footnotes
against other authors, no prescriptive patterns, an epilogue that restates the position.

**Retrieval test:** reconstruct the thesis, its strongest evidence, and its
**counterarguments**. A card with a date from this book is a card wasted.

**Plan consequence:** the output of a block is an argument map — claim, evidence,
objection, reply — written from memory and then corrected against the page. Chapters are
ordered by the argument's logic, not by the plan's convenience.

### Reference — it is not read

Manuals, cookbooks, dictionaries, API guides. **The classification's job here is to
prevent study.** The correct plan is: index it, learn where things are, consult on
demand. Reading it linearly is the error.

## Mixed books

Most real books are mixed, and the honest answer names the mix:

> "Conceptual, with a procedural tail in chapters 5-7: the criteria come first and the
> techniques assume them."

The rule: the **dominant** type sets the retrieval test for the whole book; the tail sets
the format of the output for those chapters. A book that is half conceptual and half
procedural but is studied as one is studied badly in one half.

## The three traps

1. **Treating a hard book as a good one.** Difficulty is not depth. A conceptual book
   that never gives a criterion has nothing to apply, and no plan can fix that.
2. **Treating every book as worth studying.** Deciding *not* to study a book, and giving
   a reason, is part of the method.
3. **Classifying by the reader's wish instead of the text.** A reader who wants
   procedures from a book of arguments will memorize its anecdotes and miss the
   argument. Classify what is printed, then tell them the gap.

## Evidence you must be able to cite

Before stating a classification, have in hand:

- the page where the book states its purpose (preface or introduction);
- one page that shows the type's signals;
- the contents, or an explicit note that the book prints none.

A classification without these three is a guess about a book the reader already owns —
and they can check it in thirty seconds.
